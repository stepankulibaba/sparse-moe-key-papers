Data.
We experiment with the German-English (De-
En), English-German (En-De) and English-French (En-
Fr) translation directions.
All models are trained on a mix of generic data
(Paracrawl, 1) and several seen domains:
• Medical (European Medicines Agency documents,
27);
• Law (JRC-Acquis legislative EU texts, 25);
• Ted (TED talks transcriptions, 22);
• Subtitles (OpenSubtitles dataset, 17);
• Patents (PatTR dataset, 31);
• Europarl (European Parliament Proceedings);
• News-commentary;
• Wikititles4.
4https://linguatools.org/tools/corpora/
wikipedia-parallel-titles-corpora/
The selection of seen domains is motivated by having
domains of different sizes and having a pair of close
domains, TED Talks and Subtitles (they both relate
to spoken language). We also test the generalization
capabilities of models on a set of unseen domains:
• IT (manuals and localization files of open-source
software:
GNOME, KDE, PHP, Ubuntu, and
OpenOffice);
• Koran;
• Flores (Wikipedia sentences, 18);
• Patents (medical);
• Subtitles (medical);
• Medical abstracts (MuchMore dataset5);
• European Central Bank documentation (ECB, 28,
used only for En-Fr);
• World bank data (used only for En-Fr).
Medical abstracts, Patents (medical), and Subtitles
(medical) are obtained from the UFAL Medical Corpus
v. 1.06, and all the medical examples from this corpus
are excluded from the training data. The purpose of
introducing Medical abstracts, Patents (medical), and
Subtitles (medical) domains is to test models’ capabili-
ties to recognize unseen domains related to some seen
domains (Medical, Patents, and Subtitles). Europarl,
News-commentary, Medical, Law, Ted, Subtitles, IT, Ko-
ran, and ECB data is a part of the OPUS collection [28].
Tables 7 and 8 list data statistics. During training all the
models except the experiment reported in Section 5.1,
we sample the generic domain with probability 50%
and other domains with probabilities proportional to
their size in terms of line pairs.
Training.
All the models are trained from scratch for
100k updates in mixed precision on four A100 or V100
GPUs, with a batch size of maximum 4000 tokens and
accumulated gradients over 16 batches. We do not use
early stopping, as usually all validation curves continue
growing until the end of the training. Only data paral-
lelism is used, model parallelism is not needed as all
the models fit into a single GPU’s memory. We use the
Adam optimizer with the inverse square root learning
rate schedule, with a warmup of 4k steps and a max-
imum learning rate of 0.001. The data is tokenized
using Byte Pair Encoding with a vocabulary of size 24K
for English-German and 16K for English-French.

B. Additional results for the setting with
wrong domain labels
Tables 9 and 10 show performance on "Medical" and
"Law" domains, when decoding with various domain
labels. The tables confirm the conclusion made in the
main text that using the proposed domain randomisa-
tion improves model robustness to wrong domain labels
at inference time.
C. Analysis of gate statistics.
To better assess difference between various gating
mechanisms, we compare expert activations across
different datasets of SMoE, SMoE+domain tags and
SMoE+domain aware gate, SMoE + Domain Spec gate
with DR and without.
Figure 3 plots the cosine similarity between the experts
activation vectors for each pair of test sets. Following
[14] we compute the top-1 activity metric for each ex-
pert as a fraction of tokens routed to this expert as the
first choice. Each test set is then represented by a vec-
tor containing top-1 activities of all the model’s experts
5https://muchmore.dfki.de/resources1.htm, available only for
En-De.
6https://ufal.mff.cuni.cz/ufal_medical_corpus
(10 experts × 6 amount of layers with experts). The
similarity between these vectors would indicate the
similarities in experts’ activation during decoding. In
this figure we use the generic domain embedding for all
the datasets during decoding. Therefore, it reflects how
does an access to the domain knowledge during train-
ing impacts models’ capacity to specialize its experts
based solely on token representation 𝑥.
In the second analysis, we decode a single dataset (Sub-
titles) with different domain labels. This allows to as-
sess how the domain embedding impacts the choice of
experts at inference. Figure 2 plots similarities of the
resulting experts’ activation between different domain
tags.
We see that the models without DR rely much more on
the domain tag (Figure 2) compared to models with
DR. Models with DR in its turn rely more on token
representations to specialize experts compared to the
model without DR (Figure 3). We note that models
with Dom. Aware Gate and Dom. Specific Gate (right)
lead to more dissimilar (and therefore more domain-
specialized) experts overall compared to SMoE and
SMoE+Domain.Tags models. Model with no access to
domain knowledge (SMoE) specializes its tokens more
compared to models that have access to domain labels
without DR (top part of the graph).
In addition, we note that all the models benefit from
knowledge transfer by activating similar experts on sim-
ilar domains: "Patents" and "Patents (medical)", "Subti-
tles" and "Subtitles (medical)", "Subtitles" and "Ted".
